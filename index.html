AI operations and conversational AI

I owned what a US carrier's AI told its support team to say to customers, and the process for changing it safely.

I spent seventeen months at US Mobile, a US mobile carrier with about a million customers. I started on the support floor and ended up running the AI side of its customer support.

The AI drafted replies for support agents, in chat and email. I authored and owned the playbooks it followed, for a team of more than 300 agents working around the clock. The operation handled about 178,000 AI-assisted chats a month.

What it changed: customers got quicker, warmer and more accurate answers across a large product range, agents spent less time per conversation, and the knowledge base and Help Center became easier to follow in real time. I do not publish CSAT or resolution-time figures here, and I am glad to talk them through.

Below are three things I do, each with one example, and a fourth example of starting from nothing.

There is also an interactive walk-through of how the system worked.

01

Governing AI output

I authored and owned the guidance an AI system used to draft replies, in chat and email. I also set up the release process every later change went through.

That guidance covered how a reply sounds as well as what it says. I drafted two revisions of the email writing guidelines, wrote chat conventions into every chat playbook, and reworked and maintained the system prompt for Smart Reply, the in-house assistant.

Example: the first live audit

Situation
The AI's output had never been checked systematically against live conversations.
What I did
I tested production conversations for wrong answers, broken hand-offs and routing errors. I recorded 59 findings and ranked them by severity. Then I coordinated one release across 24 playbooks, with every change tested first.
Result
All 59 findings were addressed in a single controlled release. The same steps became the release process for later changes.
What it shows
I audit a whole system, not single errors, and I change it in a controlled way.
02

Checking AI work before customers see it

Reviewing and releasing changes was a routine, frequent part of the job. I check each change against the live text and against approved facts. This is one example.

Example: a package of 233 proposed changes

Situation
A package of 233 changes was proposed across the whole chat library: 35 playbooks.
What I did
I checked that every proposed change matched the live text exactly. I checked each factual claim against internal guides, using a 37-item verification sheet. I separated the safe edits from the unsupported ones.
Result
200 verified changes shipped, across 34 of the 35 playbooks. The changes that invented prices and promo codes were stopped before any customer saw them.
What it shows
I evaluate AI output and keep a person reviewing automated work, as a matter of routine.
03

Designing for how people really behave

I measure how a tool is used before I change it.

Example: the guidance nobody reached

Situation
The newer AI playbooks were written as scenarios. Most instructions sat behind buttons a support agent had to press before the AI would draft. Drafts were often missing.
What I did
I audited the live chat playbooks and counted. 19 of 30 instruction blocks, 63%, sat behind buttons most agents never pressed. In the older format it was 2 of 40. A missing draft is worse than a wrong draft, because nobody sees it fail. So I redesigned the playbooks so every draft works with no agent input.
Result
Four playbooks were converted first, at management's direction. The approach was then applied across the set. The change in draft rate afterwards was not measured.
What it shows
Measurement before opinion, and design for real behaviour.
04

Starting from nothing: AI drafting for email

Situation
Email support had no AI drafting on the support platform.
What I did
I designed one response framework, authored seven playbooks on it, piloted them with support agents and adjusted.
Result
Within two weeks it was covering more than 6,000 emails a month. 8 in 10 accepted drafts went out without edits. I was promoted to Lead the same month.
What it shows
I can take a new AI channel from nothing to daily use.

How I work

Four steps, in this order.

  1. TestEvery change is tested against live conversations before it goes out.
  2. RankFindings are ranked by severity.
  3. LogA changelog, a decisions log and a register of approved facts. Anyone can see what changed, when and why.
  4. ReleaseChanges go out together, in one coordinated release.
