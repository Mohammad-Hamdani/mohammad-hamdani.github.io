I owned what a US carrier's AI told its support team to say to customers, and the process for changing it safely.

At US Mobile, a US carrier with about a million customers, I authored and owned the playbooks behind AI-drafted support replies across chat and email. I then adapted them for Smart Reply, the in-house assistant that also reads live account data. This page shows how that system worked, how I kept it accurate, and how I handed it over.

What it changed: customers got quicker, warmer and more accurate answers across a large product range, agents spent less time per conversation, and the knowledge base and Help Center became easier to follow in real time. I do not publish CSAT or resolution-time figures here, and I am glad to talk them through.

01 · The systemPlaybook architecture

One routing contract: every customer intent has exactly one owner.

Auto Assist picks a playbook by reading what the customer types. When two playbooks both claim a question, it blends them and invents. So I organised the chat library into eleven intent categories and gave every intent a single owning playbook. Pick a case below and follow the thread.

Rules that keep routes clean

  • Where the customer is outranks everything. Abroad and offline always goes to roaming troubleshooting.
  • Buying is never troubleshooting, wherever the customer is standing.
  • "We don't offer that" and "not on your plan" are different answers with different owners.
  • Only one playbook may hold a generic entry like "my watch".
  • A product's price lives with the product. A code that discounts it lives with offers.
02 · Case APlaybooks, not documents

Having the knowledge isn't the same as the AI being able to use it.

A playbook is a set of instructions the AI follows when it drafts. It encodes the customer's situation, the diagnostic branches, the conditions that unlock each answer, the approved wording, the exceptions and the hand-offs. The hardest problems I addressed were structural, not wording.

30 instruction blocks in a scenario-format playbook. 19 of them only appeared after an agent pressed a button.

63%

of agent actions were gated behind buttons most agents never pressed.

Playbooks were written as scenarios: the agent clicks "Completed" or "Not completed", and the right instructions unlock. In practice most agents never clicked. So for most conversations, the AI drafted from the wrong branch or produced nothing.

I counted it, then rebuilt the format so a useful draft appears with zero agent input. Conditions are written into the playbook as plain gates ("only when confirmed") that the AI can read from the conversation itself.

"A missing draft is worse than a wrong draft, because nobody sees it fail."

The working principle for the library. Four playbooks were converted first, at management's direction, then the rest. The change in draft rate afterwards was not measured.

03 · VoiceThe communication layer

An accurate answer that reads cold is still a bad answer.

Facts were half the job. Every draft also passed through a communication layer: rules for how a reply sounds, how long it runs and what it never asks. I authored the guidelines for email and chat, and played a major part in the system prompt that set Smart Reply's voice. The chat and email demos below follow these rules.

Principles only In my own words. No guideline text or prompt text appears here.

Email communication guidelines

US Mobile's standard for every email draft. I authored them.

  • Warmth and ownership in the first line
  • The real steps in the reply, never only a link
  • One complete reply, with numbered steps
  • Never ask for what the customer already sent

See them in the email demo

Chat communication guidelines

I authored them and built them into every chat playbook, because chat is a paced conversation, not a document.

  • Two or three sentences a message, longer answers split in two
  • Never more than two questions at once
  • Every "we can't" comes with what we can do instead
  • If the customer says "one sec", acknowledge once and wait

See them in the chat demo

Smart Reply's system prompt

The prompt set how the in-house assistant sounded on every draft. I played a major part in it and made a key update to it on my own.

  • Three live complaints: account details crammed into greetings, replies too long, a cold tone
  • Each traced to specific lines in the prompt
  • Addressed with six edits

05 · EmailContext over templates

The third email about the same problem shouldn't read like the first.

I ran AI drafting for the email team too, on a separate set of nine playbooks with their own conventions. Email exposes lazy writing fast, because the customer has time to reread it. Structure is what lets a draft be warm, specific and useful at once. Every draft here follows the email communication guidelines I authored.

Email communication guidelines

The standard I authored for every email draft.

  • Warmth and ownership in the first line
  • Never ask for something the customer already sent
  • One clear next action, as numbered steps
  • Offer a done-for-you path on repeat contacts
  • No em dashes, approved closing

Structured draft

Playbook, guidelines and case context

To: Daniel OkaforRe: Number STILL not transferred

Hi Daniel,

You're absolutely right that this should have been done days ago, and I want to get it sorted for you today.1

I've checked in directly with our porting team.2 Your details came through correctly both times. The transfer is on hold because your previous carrier hasn't released the number yet.3

The quickest way to get it moving:4

  1. Contact your previous carrier and share your Port Order Number: PON 48213-NL.
  2. Ask them to release the number for transfer.

That should do the trick, and I'll email you the moment the transfer completes. Your old service stays active until then, so your number keeps working in the meantime.5

If you'd rather not handle it yourself, I can set up a callback with one of our voice specialists. They'll start a 3-way call with your previous carrier and take care of the release with you. Just reply with a good number and a time between 9 AM and 9 PM ET.6

I know this has taken longer than it should have, and I really appreciate your patience while we get it done together.7

We're looking forward to hearing from you!

Best,

  1. 1Agrees with him first, in his terms. Warmth before process.
  2. 2First person and specific: who was checked, instead of "our team is looking into it".
  3. 3Clears him, then names the real cause in plain words.
  4. 4One action he can take today, as numbered steps that survive email rendering.
  5. 5A commitment the team can keep, plus a fact that lowers the stakes.
  6. 6A done-for-you path, because this is his third contact.
  7. 7Closes on the relationship. Patience is acknowledged, not demanded.

What changes with account context

Auto Assist can only route on Maya's words, and her words describe a customer at home. Smart Reply checks the network first, sees her on a partner network in Canada, re-routes to roaming troubleshooting and gives the opposite instruction. That is the whole case for account access: the fix depends on where the customer is, and customers rarely say.

The flip side, which shaped how we tested it: a wrong answer wrapped in real account data is more convincing, so agents accept it more. Playbook quality decides whether account access is an accuracy gain or a confident miss.

08 · Case CReliability and release

A change to an AI playbook isn't copy-editing. It's a release.

One word in the wrong place changes what thousands of drafts say. So every change went through the same loop: find the pattern, name the structural cause, make one precise change, prove it, and record it. Click through the loop.

A real example of a structural cause

Drafts kept quoting the wrong price for the top plan. The obvious answer was to add more pricing text. I checked what the AI was reading instead: the plans playbook, which held the right price, had no pricing words in its opening, so price questions never reached it. The cause was routing, not content. Recurrence afterwards was not formally tracked.

Handover

Continuity

On the floor

One of the shift leads on a 24/7 support floor of 300+ agents.

Escalations, approvals and release training. The playbooks I authored and owned were in use on every shift.

How I lead change

  • People adopt a tool when someone they trust shows them why it helps. Training came with every release.
  • Batch the evidence before acting. One anomaly is not a pattern.
  • Separate my decisions from other owners' domains, and route proposals rather than overwrite.
  • Write it down so the next person doesn't need me.
